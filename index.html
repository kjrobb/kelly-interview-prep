Kelly Robb

Evergreen Interview Prep

Tell me about yourself.

I’ve spent most of my career helping established, mission-driven organizations keep their value relevant as their audiences and business goals evolve. At The Oregonian, that meant helping a legacy journalism brand adapt to digital behavior, grow new audiences, and strengthen the connection between its journalism and the community it served. At Multnomah Athletic Club, it meant helping a 130-year-old membership organization modernize how it attracted, onboarded, engaged, and retained members while protecting what made membership valuable. The common thread has been helping organizations understand what their audiences need now, then evolving the marketing and experience in ways that strengthen engagement, loyalty, and growth.

What are your greatest strengths?

One of my biggest strengths is taking something complicated and making it clear. I’m good at looking across the customer, the data, the business goals, and the teams involved, figuring out what actually matters, and turning that into a plan people can execute. I’m also comfortable moving between strategy and execution, so I stay close enough to the work to know whether what we planned is actually working.

How would you describe your leadership style?

My leadership style is clear, collaborative, and individualized. I want people to understand what we’re trying to accomplish and have real ownership over their work, and I adjust how I communicate and support people based on how they work best. I don’t want people waiting for me to make every decision. I want to give them the context and clarity to make good decisions themselves.

How do you manage stakeholders?

I start by making sure we agree on what we’re trying to accomplish, who needs to be involved, what decisions need to be made, and where the dependencies are. I try to surface disagreements and risks early rather than discovering them halfway through execution. From there, I keep communication predictable and ownership clear so everyone understands what they’re responsible for and what they need from each other.

How do you handle conflict or disagreement?

I try to understand what the other person is actually trying to accomplish or protect before I argue for my solution. Usually there’s a shared objective even when we disagree about how to get there. I bring the conversation back to that objective, clarify what we actually need to decide, and use data or agreed-upon criteria where we can. That usually gets us out of competing opinions and back into solving the problem.

Tell me about a time you disagreed with a stakeholder.

We had stakeholders who wanted parents included in communications to adult members approaching an important membership transition. I understood the reasoning because involving the family could potentially improve conversion, but I was concerned about assuming an adult member wanted their family involved. Rather than simply saying no, I proposed that we build an explicit communication preference into the process going forward. It preserved the opportunity without creating a privacy issue in the current campaign.

Tell me about a time something didn’t go according to plan.

We were implementing a major database change and realized after the project started that we hadn’t captured the full requirements from every department using the system. Rather than push forward and create bigger problems later, I helped pause the project, bring the right stakeholders together, clarify the requirements and risks, and reset the timeline. We moved forward with much stronger alignment, and it changed how I approach complex projects. I now surface dependencies and requirements much earlier.

Tell me about a successful project.

One I’m particularly proud of was redesigning our new-member onboarding. Our research showed that members were still figuring out how to use the club four or five months after joining, so a one-time orientation clearly wasn’t enough. I redesigned onboarding as a 12-month experience with targeted communications, events, and engagement opportunities throughout the first year. Email engagement increased from roughly 60% to 80%, and participation in new-member events more than doubled.

Tell me about your retention experience.

I wanted us to get better at identifying retention risk before someone actually resigned. I analyzed check-in behavior alongside resignation reasons and found that declining usage could give us an earlier signal of disengagement. We tested targeted communications and personal outreach before those members reached the point of leaving, and during the pilot we reduced resignations attributed to non-use by about 10%. It shifted our retention strategy from reacting to cancellations to intervening earlier.

Tell me about your acquisition or conversion experience.

We had strong demand, but we weren’t converting enough of that demand into membership. I looked at where prospects were stalling and found opportunities to improve follow-up, re-engage prospects at key stages, and better involve the members who had referred them. Those changes helped increase conversion from roughly 75% to 95%.

How do you use data to make decisions?

I start with the business question rather than the report. I want to understand what we’re trying to learn or decide, and then determine which data actually helps answer that. I also look at both outcomes and the behaviors leading to them. If we can see customer behavior changing before it shows up in revenue, conversion, or retention, we have an opportunity to act instead of simply reporting what already happened.

What metrics do you look at?

It depends on the business question. For acquisition, I’m looking at pipeline, conversion, and where people are dropping out. For engagement, I’m looking at participation, usage, and response behavior. For retention, I’m looking at attrition alongside the behaviors that precede it. And I always want to connect those measures back to the business outcome rather than measuring activity for its own sake.

Tell me about something you built from scratch.

I built a Membership Health Framework because leadership had a lot of individual metrics but no single way to understand what they meant together. I brought acquisition, resignations, dues performance, replacement ratios, pipeline, and long-term trends into one framework so we could see where the business was healthy, where risk was developing, and where we needed to act. It was ultimately adopted by leadership and the Board as part of how we evaluated membership performance.

Tell me about a complex cross-functional project.

One example was a systems issue between Membership and Technology where we had different views about what needed to change. I led a working session that moved us away from debating individual tools and toward agreeing on what the solution actually needed to accomplish around member experience, data, privacy, and functionality. From there, I developed several options we could evaluate against those requirements, and we ultimately landed on a solution that addressed both teams’ needs.

How do you approach a new marketing problem?

I start with the business outcome we’re trying to change and what we know about the audience. Then I look at the data and customer behavior to understand where the actual opportunity or friction is. From there, I determine who we need to reach, what we need them to do, what message and channels make sense, and how we’ll measure whether it worked.

How do you build a marketing plan?

I start with the business goal and the audience. Then I define what we know, what behavior we’re trying to influence, the strategy and messaging, the channels and tactics, the measures of success, the timeline, and who owns what. By the time we start executing, everyone should understand what we’re trying to accomplish, how we’re going to do it, and how we’ll know whether it worked.

Are you more of a marketer or a membership person?

I’m a marketer who spent the last several years applying marketing to the membership lifecycle. My role sat in Membership, but the work included acquisition strategy, segmentation, campaign planning, onboarding, engagement, retention, win-back, creative direction, and measurement. Being that close to the member experience actually made me a stronger marketer because I could see firsthand how customer behavior connected to the marketing decisions we were making.

What was your role in marketing campaigns?

I owned the strategy, audience and segmentation, campaign direction, creative brief, optimization, and performance analysis. I partnered with creative specialists on copy and design and with the appropriate teams on deployment, but I was responsible for defining what we were trying to accomplish, who we needed to reach, what the campaign needed to do, and how we would evaluate the results.

Are you strategic or hands-on?

Both. I’m comfortable setting the strategy and building the plan, but I also like staying close to execution. I’ve worked directly with segmentation, campaign development, systems, reporting, analysis, testing, and optimization. I don’t want to hand off a strategy and disappear. I want to understand whether it’s working and what we need to change.

How do you work with creative teams?

I start by making sure the creative team understands the business objective, the audience, what we know about them, and the action we’re trying to drive. I’m not a designer or copywriter, and I don’t try to be one. My role is to give them a strong strategic brief, provide useful feedback, protect the objective of the work, and give talented creative people room to solve the problem.

What do you enjoy most about marketing?

I like finding opportunities. Sometimes that starts with data and sometimes it starts with a customer problem, but I enjoy figuring out what’s happening, what we could change, and what might create a better result. Then I like bringing the right people together to actually build and test the solution.

How do you prioritize?

I look at what will have the greatest impact on the business, what is genuinely time-sensitive, and what other work depends on it. I also distinguish between something being important and something simply being loud. Once priorities are clear, I make sure the people involved understand what we’re doing now, what can wait, and why.

How do you manage multiple projects?

I get very clear about outcomes, deadlines, dependencies, owners, and decision points upfront. From there, I keep enough structure around the work that I can see where something is at risk before it becomes an emergency. I don’t believe in adding process for the sake of process. I want enough structure to keep the work moving and make responsibilities clear.

How do you work independently?

I’m very comfortable owning work independently. I like clarity around the goal and decision-making authority upfront, and then I don’t need a lot of oversight to keep something moving. I’ll bring people in when I need expertise, alignment, or a decision, but I’m comfortable figuring things out and moving the work forward.

What kind of environment do you work best in?

I do my best work in a collaborative environment with direct communication, shared goals, and clear ownership. I like teams where people can challenge ideas and solve problems together, but once we’ve made a decision, we know who’s responsible and we move.

How do you stay current?

I’m a pretty active continuous learner. I stay current through industry research, professional communities, certifications, and hands-on experimentation with new technology. I recently renewed my HubSpot digital marketing and content marketing certifications, and I use AI regularly for research, analysis, ideation, and workflow improvement. I try to stay current on the tools without losing sight of the fundamentals because customer behavior, segmentation, testing, and measurement transfer across platforms.

How do you approach technology you haven't used before?

I’m comfortable learning new platforms because I start by understanding what the technology needs to accomplish rather than memorizing where every button is. I’ve worked across CRM, communications, project management, analytics, and database systems, and I’m usually able to get productive quickly because I understand the workflows and business requirements behind the technology.

Why did you leave your last role?

My position was eliminated as part of an organizational restructuring in July. I’d been at MAC for eight years and had the opportunity to grow through several roles, so I’m proud of what I built there. It also gave me the opportunity to be thoughtful about what I want next and find a role that brings together the marketing, customer, analytical, and leadership parts of my background.

What are you looking for in your next role?

I’m looking for a role where I can own a meaningful business problem, not simply execute a channel. I want to understand the customer and the business well enough to identify where the opportunity is, have enough ownership to build the strategy, and work across teams to turn it into measurable results.

Why should we hire you?

I’m good at coming into a complex business problem, understanding what’s happening with the customer and the business, and figuring out where there’s an opportunity to make something work better. I can build the strategy, align the people needed to execute it, and stay close enough to the work to know whether it actually delivered. That combination of marketing judgment, customer understanding, and operational discipline is what I’d bring to the role.

What’s an area you’re continuing to develop?

I’ve been working on being more concise when I know a subject really well. My instinct is to give people all of the context because I want them to understand how I reached the conclusion. I’ve gotten much better at starting with the answer and then adding context based on what the person actually needs.
